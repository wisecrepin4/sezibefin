# Digital PR and outreach principles

Load this when you need to tier publications, write subject lines, or structure a pitch.

## Why a journalist opens an email

Journalists get dozens to hundreds of pitches a week. Almost all get deleted in two seconds. The ones that get opened and read have three things:

1. **A subject line with a number in it.** A specific statistic in the subject line signals the email contains a real story, not a sales pitch. "Regional shoppers spend 38% more than city shoppers" beats "Story idea for you" every time.
2. **Proof in the first two sentences that you read their work.** Not flattery. A specific reference to a recent article and what it argued. This is the single biggest difference between a pitch that lands and one that does not.
3. **The story, not the company.** The email leads with the finding. The business is mentioned once, near the end, as the source. A journalist cares about a story their readers will click. They do not care about the user's business.

## The anatomy of a pitch that works

- **Subject line:** the headline finding, with the number. Under 10 words.
- **Line 1:** reference the journalist's recent, specific work.
- **Line 2 to 3:** the finding, with the key statistic. One sentence of context on why it is surprising.
- **Line 4:** why this matters to that publication's readers.
- **Line 5:** the offer. The full data, the study, an interview or a quote, exclusive if appropriate.
- **Close:** one line, the business named as the source, a clear way to reply.
- **Length:** under 150 words total. Shorter is better.

## Publication tiers

Match the finding to the right tier. A national outlet will not run a niche trade story, and a trade outlet will not run a fluffy national one.

- **Tier 1, national and major press.** General-interest mastheads, major news sites, national broadcasters. They want a story with broad human interest or a number that surprises everyone. The bar is high. The link and reach are large.
- **Tier 2, industry trade press.** Outlets that cover one industry for the people in it. They want findings specific and useful to that industry. The bar is lower, the audience is more relevant, and these are often the easiest genuine wins.
- **Tier 3, local press and niche blogs.** Regional papers, local news sites, niche-interest blogs. They want a local angle: a finding about their town, their region, their community. Easy to place, and strong when the data has a geographic story.

A single data study can usually be pitched to all three tiers, with the angle reframed for each. The regional-spend study, for example: Tier 1 as a national consumer-economy story, Tier 2 as a retail-industry story, Tier 3 as a "your region punches above its weight" local story.

## Follow-up

One follow-up only, after about a week of no reply. Under 50 words. Reply to the original email so the thread is intact. Add one new thing: a fresh angle, an offer of exclusivity, or a deadline. Never just "did you see my email".

## What not to do

- No "I hope this email finds you well".
- No "I thought you might be interested in".
- No attachments on the first email. Offer the data, let them ask.
- No pitching a journalist who does not cover the topic. Wrong fit wastes both people's time and burns goodwill.
- No mass identical email. If two journalists get the same words, neither will run it.
