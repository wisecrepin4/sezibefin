# Information gain

Load this when you need worked examples of what genuine information gain looks like, or when the user asks why their AI-written content is not ranking.

## What information gain is

Google does not ban AI content. It cannot reliably detect it anyway. AI detectors routinely flag human writing as AI and AI writing as human, so a detector-based penalty would be unworkable.

Instead, Google rewards pages that add something new. The internal idea is information gain: the amount of genuinely new, useful information a page provides that the other ranking pages on that topic do not. A page that only restates what is already on page one has near-zero information gain. A page that adds a real result, a real number, a real test, or a real opinion has high information gain.

This matters more every year, because the easier it gets to mass-produce remixed content, the more Google leans on information gain to tell the new from the recycled.

## Why a raw AI draft scores near zero on its own

An AI model is trained on text that already exists. When it writes about a topic, it is averaging and rephrasing what has already been published. By definition, that is the opposite of new. A raw draft can be well structured, clear, and accurate, and still add nothing the internet did not already have.

That is not a flaw to fix by prompting harder. It is the nature of the tool. The fix is human input.

## The four sources of information gain a business already owns

Every business sitting down to write a page already has these. The skill's job is to pull them out.

1. **A case study or client result.** "We moved a client from a buy to a lease and their first-quarter cash position improved by X." Specific, real, theirs alone.
2. **An internal data point.** A number from their own operations, sales, or customer base that is not on the first page of Google. "Across 200 installs last year, the most common reason a cafe outgrew its machine was Y."
3. **A test or an experiment.** "We tried Z for six months. Here is what actually happened, including the part that did not work."
4. **An expert opinion that pushes back.** A view, earned from doing the work, that contradicts the common advice. Contrarian but honest beats safe and generic every time.

## What strong vs weak gaps look like in a draft

A gap marker must ask a question that only the user can answer, and that produces something specific.

Strong:
- "What is the single most common mistake you see customers make in the first month, and what does it cost them?"
- "Walk through the last time a customer chose the cheaper option against your advice. What happened?"
- "What number from your own jobs would surprise someone reading this page?"

Weak (do not write these):
- "Add a personal anecdote here."
- "Insert your thoughts."
- "Share your experience on this topic."

The weak versions get skipped or filled with more generic text. The strong versions force a real, ownable answer.

## The three-step playbook to give the user

When you explain the principle in your output, give the user the same three steps the agency uses:

1. **Interview yourself or an expert.** Spend ten minutes talking out loud about what you actually know. Record it on your phone. Get a transcript. You do not need to write well. You need to know your job.
2. **Add unique data.** Case studies, client results, your own numbers. The things only you have because you did the work.
3. **Publish naturally.** A genuinely useful page a week beats hundreds of thin pages dumped at once. Consistency from a business that has something to say always wins.

Steps 1 and 2 are what create the information gain. Step 3 is what lets Google trust it.
